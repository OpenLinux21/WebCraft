## Overview

Web pages are overwhelmingly made of words: articles, captions, prices, buttons, footnotes. Lesson 01 gave you the frame of a document; this lesson is about what goes inside it, and about the difference between text that merely _looks_ right and text that _means_ something.

The key word is _semantic_. A semantic element is one whose name describes its content: a main heading is an `h1`, a navigation menu is a `nav`, an independent story is an `article`. Compare that with the generic `div`, which means nothing at all - it is a blank box you can style. When every box on the page is a `div`, only a human looking at the rendered pixels can tell the navigation from the footer.

Semantic markup pays off the moment anything other than your eyes reads the page. Screen readers announce a `nav` as a landmark their users can jump to. Search engines weight an `h1` more than a styled `div`. Reader modes and browser extensions strip pages down to `article` and `main`. And future you can navigate a semantic file at a glance.

Here is the plan: first headings, which give a page its outline; then paragraphs and their inline friends, which carry the meaning inside the text; then the semantic containers that shape the page; and finally the exercise, where you rebuild a page that is currently nothing but divs.

## Headings: the outline of the page

Headings turn a wall of text into a document people can scan. The browser renders `h1` through `h6` in decreasing size, but the size is the least important part: headings are an _outline_, the same table of contents you would put at the front of a book.

```html
<h1>Hiking the Blue Ridge</h1>
<h2>Day one: fog, then sunshine</h2>
<h3>Morning: the switchbacks</h3>
<h3>Afternoon: the ridge line</h3>
```

Two rules keep an outline healthy. First, exactly one `h1` per page: it names the topic, and everything else nests under it. Second, never skip levels: an `h3` should live under an `h2`, which lives under the `h1` - assistive technology builds a navigation tree from your levels, and a jump from `h1` to `h4` reads as missing content.

| Level        | Typical use                                |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| `h1`         | The topic of the page, used once           |
| `h2`         | Major sections of the page                 |
| `h3`         | Subsections inside an `h2`                 |
| `h4` to `h6` | Deeper nesting - rarely needed in practice |

Choose heading levels by structure, not by size. If the browser's default `h3` is too big or too small for your design, you will fix that with CSS in the next module - never by reaching for a different level.

> **Try it:** In the built-in editor, outline a week of your life: one `h1`, three `h2` sections, and an `h3` inside one of them. Preview and watch the browser size each level for you.

## Paragraphs, breaks and dividers

The `p` element is the workhorse of the web: one idea per paragraph, as many paragraphs as you need. The browser adds vertical space between paragraphs automatically, and screen readers pause on them - both of which make long text digestible.

```html
<p>Our first day on the trail began in thick fog.</p>
<p>By noon the sun had burned it off, and the ridge appeared.</p>
```

Two neighbours complete the set. `br` forces a line break _inside_ a paragraph, and `hr` draws a divider that marks a thematic break - a change of scene, a shift of topic.

```html
<address>
  50 Forest Road<br />
  Blue Ridge, NC
</address>
<hr />
<p>Next up: day two on the trail.</p>
```

Both are easy to misuse. Reserve `br` for text where the line break is part of the content itself - poems, addresses, song lyrics - and do not use `br` or `hr` to push content around the page. Separation of ideas is an element's job; separation of pixels is CSS's job, which starts in Lesson 04.

> **Try it:** In the editor, write two paragraphs, then put an `hr` between them. Next, type a two-line address using one `br`. Notice the different rhythm: `hr` separates blocks, `br` breaks a line.

## Inline meaning: text-level elements

Inside a paragraph you often need to mark up individual words: a phrase that matters more than the rest, a stressed word, a highlighted detail. HTML has a small vocabulary of _text-level_ elements for exactly this, and each one carries meaning rather than just appearance:

| Element  | Meaning                                   | Default look      |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| `strong` | Strong importance                         | bold              |
| `em`     | Emphasis, spoken stress                   | italic            |
| `mark`   | Highlight relevant to the current context | yellow background |
| `small`  | Side comments and fine print              | smaller text      |
| `code`   | A fragment of computer code               | monospace         |

Used together in one paragraph:

```html
<p>
  Pack <strong>two liters of water</strong> - the ridge has no shade. It was <em>hotter</em> than
  the forecast promised, and my <mark>favorite pen</mark> melted. The route notes live in
  <code>route/day1.txt</code>.
</p>
```

You may also meet `b` (bold) and `i` (italic). They still exist, but they only say "draw this differently", while `strong` and `em` say _why_. When choosing, ask a simple question: would I stress this word while reading aloud? That is `em`. Is this phrase important to understand the sentence? That is `strong`. If the answer is only "I want it to look different", you probably want CSS instead.

> **Try it:** In the editor, write a paragraph with one `strong`, one `em` and one `mark`. Then preview and see how the three looks differ - and notice that the markup still makes sense with all styling removed.

## Semantic containers: from div to main

Most pages share a shape: a banner on top, navigation under it, one main content area, tangential notes at the side, a footer at the bottom. HTML gives each of these regions a purpose-built element:

| Element   | Role                                                 |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| `header`  | Introductory content: site name, logo, tagline       |
| `nav`     | Major navigation links                               |
| `main`    | The dominant content of the page - only one per page |
| `section` | A thematic group, usually with its own heading       |
| `article` | Content complete on its own: a post, a news item     |
| `aside`   | Tangential content: a sidebar, a pull quote          |
| `footer`  | Footer information: author, copyright, links         |
| `div`     | A neutral box for when nothing else fits             |

Assembled into a small blog page:

```html
<header>
  <p>My Travel Blog</p>
  <nav>Home | Posts | About</nav>
</header>
<main>
  <article>
    <h1>Hiking the Blue Ridge</h1>
    <h2>Day one: fog, then sunshine</h2>
    <p>The trail started in fog and ended in sunshine.</p>
  </article>
  <aside>
    <p>This week: three posts from the trail.</p>
  </aside>
</main>
<footer>
  <p>Written by Sam</p>
</footer>
```

Note that `div` is not banned - it is the honest choice when a grouping has no meaning, such as a wrapper that exists purely for styling. The rule of thumb: pick the element whose name matches the content, and reach for `div` only when no name fits.

> **Try it:** In the editor, rebuild this blog shape with your own words. The preview will barely look different - that is expected. The structure is now machine-readable, which is the whole point.

## Why semantics matter

Three audiences read your markup, and only one of them sees pixels. People using assistive technology get a map: `nav` is a landmark they can jump to, headings are a skimmable outline, `main` tells them where the content starts. Search engines get clues about what matters on the page. And future you gets a file that explains itself.

Contrast the two versions of a menu:

```html
<div class="menu">Home | Posts | About</div>
```

```html
<nav>Home | Posts | About</nav>
```

To a browser they render identically. To everything else, the first line is noise and the second is a labeled landmark. With the `nav` version, a screen reader user hears "navigation" and can skip straight to it or past it; with the `div` version, they must listen to the whole page.

There is a maintenance bonus too. Semantic markup reads almost like a diagram of the page, so when you return to a file months later - or when a teammate does - the structure is visible without running anything.

> **Try it:** In the editor, open the solution file of this lesson's exercise and read only the element names, top to bottom. You can reconstruct what the page is about without reading a single sentence of content - that is what semantic HTML buys you.

## The exercise: from div soup to meaning

The starter page is a tiny travel blog written entirely with `div` elements and class names. It renders, and it even looks reasonable - but to every machine reading it, the page is four meaningless boxes.

Rebuild it following the TODO markers:

1. Turn the banner `div` into a `header`, and the menu `div` into a `nav`.
2. Wrap the story zone in `main`, and the post itself in `article`.
3. Fix the heading hierarchy: the big title becomes the page's only `h1`, and the subtitle becomes an `h2`.
4. Inside the paragraph, mark _worth every step_ with `strong` and highlight _fog_ with `mark`.
5. Turn the byline `div` into a `footer`.

Preview as you go and you will notice something surprising: the page looks the same. That is not a bug - semantics is invisible to the eye and visible to machines. What should change is how the document reads: one `h1`, no skipped levels, and every region with a name.

When you finish, compare your file with the solution. The exact words will differ from yours, but the element names should match one for one.

> **Tip:** If you are unsure which element fits, say out loud what the content is: "this is the site banner" is a `header`; "this is the story" is an `article`; "this is just a box" stays a `div`.

## Common Mistakes

### 1. More than one h1 on a page

Two `h1` elements tell screen readers and search engines that the page has two topics - which means it has none.

Wrong:

```html
<h1>My Travel Blog</h1>
<h1>Hiking the Blue Ridge</h1>
```

Right:

```html
<h1>Hiking the Blue Ridge</h1>
<h2>Day one: fog, then sunshine</h2>
```

### 2. Skipping heading levels

Levels exist to nest; jumping from `h1` straight to `h3` makes the outline look like content is missing.

Wrong:

```html
<h1>Hiking the Blue Ridge</h1>
<h3>Day one</h3>
```

Right:

```html
<h1>Hiking the Blue Ridge</h1>
<h2>Day one</h2>
```

### 3. Using br to fake paragraphs

A run of `br` elements looks like paragraphs but reads as one; the browser cannot tell ideas apart.

Wrong:

```html
<p>First thought.<br />Second thought.<br />Third thought.</p>
```

Right:

```html
<p>First thought.</p>
<p>Second thought.</p>
<p>Third thought.</p>
```

### 4. Using b and i when you mean strong and em

The old visual elements say nothing about importance, so tools cannot tell a keyword from a stylistic whim.

Wrong:

```html
<p>This is <b>important</b> and <i>really</i> matters.</p>
```

Right:

```html
<p>This is <strong>important</strong> and <em>really</em> matters.</p>
```

### 5. Wrapping everything in section

Reaching for `section` on every block is div soup in new clothes; use it only when the group has a heading and a theme.

Wrong:

```html
<section>My Travel Blog</section>
<section>Home | Posts</section>
```

Right:

```html
<header>My Travel Blog</header>
<nav>Home | Posts</nav>
```

### 6. Choosing elements by their default look

Picking `h3` because it is small, or `blockquote` because it indents, bakes browser styling into your structure and breaks the moment you restyle.

Wrong:

```html
<blockquote>The sidebar links</blockquote>
```

Right:

```html
<aside>The sidebar links</aside>
```

> **Tip:** The preview cannot catch semantic mistakes - it looks fine either way. Read your markup back to yourself instead: every element name should be a word you would use to describe the page.

## Summary

- Headings `h1` to `h6` are the page's outline: one `h1`, no skipped levels.
- `p` carries paragraphs; `br` breaks a line inside one; `hr` marks a thematic break.
- `strong`, `em`, `mark`, `small` and `code` add meaning to individual words - prefer them over `b` and `i`.
- `header`, `nav`, `main`, `section`, `article`, `aside` and `footer` name the regions of a page; `div` is the neutral fallback.
- Semantic markup looks identical in the preview but is readable by screen readers, search engines and future you.
- You rebuilt a page of anonymous divs into a document whose every region has a name.
